## The problem

TV products in the Virgin Media existing customer store sat behind one TV accordion as a long list. Customers had to scroll through it to find what they wanted. Products weren't grouped by genre and core TV packages looked much like add-ons.

User research had already shown that people don't like scrolling to find information. They also skip products they don't understand or see the value of. TV engagement and attachment were low compared with broadband, which matters because customers with more products are more likely to stay.

Earlier testing with customers pointed to larger cards with clearer calls to action. The question for CRO was whether that would change what people actually ordered.

## What we tested

The test ran from 9 to 23 September 2026 with in-contract customers who had TV cross-sell or upsell options. Traffic was split three ways.

- **Control:** the existing TV accordion.
- **Variation 1:** the new product cards.
- **Variation 2:** the new product cards plus genre filter chips.

The primary metric was XSUS orders, the share of visitors who placed a cross-sell or upsell order.

## The headline result

| | Order rate | Uplift | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Control | 2.2% | | |
| Variation 1: cards | 2.5% | 14% | 90% |
| Variation 2: cards + chips | **2.7%** | **21%** | **95%** |

Both variations beat the existing accordion. Variation 2 did best and was the only one to reach 95% confidence. Because the cards were the same in both, the gap between them shows what the filter chips added on their own.

The uplift was spread evenly. Every TV product tracked in Variation 2 saw about 21% to 28% more orders at 95% confidence, from Mega TV to Sky Sports HD to Kids. The redesign lifted the whole TV range rather than one popular product.

## Fewer clicks, more orders

The most interesting finding went against what I expected. **Variation 2 had nearly 10% fewer add-to-basket clicks than Variation 1** but more orders.

Some popular products were added to basket less often in Variation 2, such as Mixit TV (over a third fewer) and Sky UHD. Yet Mixit TV orders were up about 28%.

My reading is that people in Variation 2 found the right product sooner and added it with more intent. People clicked less but bought more.

## People used the chips

The chips weren't ignored. Sports was the most used filter by far.

| Filter | Share of visitors who used it |
|---|---|
| Sports | about 4% |
| Movies | about 1.5% |
| Show all | about 1% |
| Kids | under 1% |
| International | under 1% |

Sports got almost three times as many clicks as Movies. That backs up what came out of the research: customers think about what they want to watch first, so grouping by genre matches how they shop.

## No harm elsewhere

- **Containment stayed flat** at around 86% with no significant change, so the redesign didn't push more people to contact Virgin Media for help.
- **Revenue rose about 23%** in Variation 2 compared with the Control during the test.
- **Average revenue per order rose almost 4%.** Variation 1 had a higher average order, but Variation 2 brought in more orders and more revenue overall.

## What happens next

Variation 2 is being rolled out as the new TV experience. The next step is testing the same card pattern on non-TV products such as broadband, bundles, SIM and WiFi Pods. That will show whether the hierarchy, comparison and selection principles hold beyond TV.

## What I'd take away

- **I'd have shipped the wrong version on clicks alone.** Add-to-basket made Variation 2 look worse. Checking orders changed the decision.
- **Test the parts separately.** Running cards alone against cards with chips showed what each change was worth, instead of one result for the whole bundle.
- **Organise around how people think.** The chips worked because they matched a pattern research had already found. Customers start from what they want to watch.
